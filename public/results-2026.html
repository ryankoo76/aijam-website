The judges' report

Four criteria · 100 points

Every entry was scored on the same four criteria, published in the guidebook before entries opened. This is what the panel saw across the whole field — not only the top of it.

The eleventh edition was the most technically confident field we have judged — and the most honest about its own limits.

Teams stopped describing AI as a feature and started describing it as a component with a job: where the model reads, where the database decides, where a human still has to approve. Entries that drew that line clearly scored well on every criterion. Entries that blurred it lost points on Clarity before the panel ever reached Feasibility.

Healthcare was the largest category by a wide margin, and within it the strongest work was not diagnostic — it was access. Screening by SMS. Verification from a phone photo. A first check that happens before the clinic, not instead of it. Environment and Social Good followed the same instinct: build for the constraint the user is already living with.

Where the field was weakest was Feasibility. Many teams described a system that would work if the data, the hardware and the partnership all arrived. The panel rewarded the smaller number who told us what they had actually built, what broke, and what they left for next year.

How teams used AI in 2026

Panel analysis of the judged entries

After scoring was finished, the panel went back and asked a separate question: how far does each team actually understand its own AI? Not how good the idea was — what the team could prove it knew. The answer split the field into five clean steps, and those steps turned out to predict the ranking almost exactly.

Level 0
No AI present — the entry calls itself AI but contains none. Sometimes the submission says so itself: "inspired by AI". 6% of entries
Level 1
Naming — "the AI analyses", "machine learning predicts". A verb with no object. Which model, trained on what, wrong when? All blank. The largest group. 42% of entries
Level 2
Task defined — the team narrowed exactly what the AI must solve. "Separate one instrument from an ensemble recording." "Segment each tooth in under 0.3 seconds." They know what to ask for, not yet how to get it. 21% of entries
Level 3
Stack named — real models, real data, real constraints, by name. "YOLOv8n + MediaPipe Pose, on-device on a Raspberry Pi 4." "OpenCV + EasyOCR against a live regulatory database." 12% of entries
Level 4
Measured — the team held data back from training, ran their own system against it, and reported the number. "597 held-out messages, 270 of them normal text written to look like fraud." "2,428 detections over 15 km of road footage, 85.6%." 18% of entries
The finding of the year

The field does not need to be taught more about AI. The teams at Level 1 knew exactly what they wanted to build. What they were missing was not knowledge — it was the habit of measuring what they had made. That is a one-afternoon skill, and it moved teams up two tiers.

The top of the field gave their AI less authority, not more

Six teams designed around a different question — not "what can the AI do here" but "what must the AI never be allowed to decide". Those six averaged 87.3 against the field's 74.6, and five of them finished in the top nine. Restraint turned out to be the most reliable evidence of understanding: you cannot fence off the part where a model goes wrong unless you know where that is.

This year the work crossed a line

From invention to product

For ten editions this competition collected inventions — good ideas, explained well. In 2026 something changed, and the panel could see it in the submissions themselves. Teams did not stop at the idea. They built the thing, pointed it at real data, measured what came back, and said what it got wrong. That is not a science-fair project. That is the first version of a product.

What an entry used to be

An invention, described

  • The buildA concept, and a demo that worked once, in the room
  • The AI“Our system uses AI to analyse the data”
  • The dataA sample the team collected by hand
  • AccuracyNot measured
  • Cost to buildA lab, a supervisor, a research budget
  • Next stepA poster at the next science fair
What an entry is in 2026

A working product, measured

  • The buildA running system — a web app, a phone app, a device that patrols a room
  • The AINamed models doing named jobs: YOLOv8n for detection, OCR for the label, a language model held to retrieved facts
  • The dataLive public sources — satellite imagery, drug registries, weather APIs
  • AccuracyTested against data held back from training, reported with its failure cases
  • Cost to buildA laptop, a phone, and in some cases a $60 single-board computer
  • Next stepA first customer

Every top-ranked entry was assembled from models that already exist in public. The hard part was no longer building the intelligence. It was deciding what to point it at.

Everything they built with is public, documented and mostly free

These are the actual components named in this year's submissions. Not one of them requires a research lab, a licence negotiation or a data-centre. This is the whole reason the line got crossed: AI stopped being something you build and became something you assemble.

What this means for anyone reading

The teams on this page are between fourteen and nineteen years old. They did not have privileged access to models, data or compute — they used the same public parts that are available to you today. The distance between an idea and a product that someone can buy is now measured in weekends, not degrees. That is the real result of 2026, and it belongs to the whole field, not only to the winners.

What the field worked on

By problem type, not by category

Regrouped by the shape of the problem rather than the subject label, the concentration is striking. Fraud, falls, floods, maternal risk, counterfeit medicine, road hazards, wounds, fire, drunk driving, Braille defects, retinopathy, dyslexia — all of them are one problem: tell someone before the bad thing happens.

Method — total scores follow the published guidebook weighting (Impact 30 · Clarity 25 · Novelty 25 · Feasibility 20). The five understanding levels are the panel's assessment of each submission's own text and verified links, not an automated measurement.

Grand Prix · Vietnam

Khoan Đã

Nguyen Xuan Minh Quan

AI-application Social Good Level 4 · measured
The problem

A scam needs about sixty seconds of an older person's trust. Named after the Vietnamese for "hold on", the app is built for exactly that minute.

The answer

Paste a message, a screenshot or a link. The system names the warning signs it can see and offers one button — and it never tells the user they are safe, because the absence of a known signal is not proof of safety.

Why the judges chose it

Two things put this first. It measured itself honestly — 597 held-back messages, 270 of them ordinary text deliberately written to look like fraud, which is the hard half of the test most teams skip. And it solved prompt injection with architecture rather than filtering: the model's output has no verdict field, so an instruction hidden in a scam message has nowhere to go. That is the single best design decision in this year's field.

Entering in 2027?

Do one thing the 2026 field mostly did not: hold some data back, run your own system against it, and put the number in your submission — including what it gets wrong. That single habit separated the top eleven from everyone else.
